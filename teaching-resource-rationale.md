# Teaching Resource Rationale

This [Year 7 geometry resource](teaching-resources.md) combines teacher-narrated animations, whole-class discussion, differentiated exercises and digital submission. It targets [AC9M7M04 and AC9M7M05](https://www.australiancurriculum.edu.au/resources/work-samples/mathematics/year-7/ws01-geometric-reasoning/) on angles on parallel lines and triangles, as well as aims to build mathematical reasoning skills and procedural fluency. This resource operates around *Assessment in the Digital Age* to form a formative cycle where students submit reasoning and steps for exercises through the school’s digital system, followed by the teacher’s targeted feedback to check for misconceptions and weaknesses. In doing so, the digital platform holds evidence of students’ reasoning for the teacher to adapt further teaching strategies.

## Conceptual understanding and discovery

The resource prioritises the derivation of new concepts, in this case, angles, from previous learning using digital animations. It avoids the approach that asks students to memorise a new set of rules. For instance, the animation first introduces supplementary angles on a straight line and derives the vertically opposite angle property. During the animation, the teacher will narrate each step and pause for predictions and explanations. [Mayer and Moreno’s (2003)](https://doi.org/10.1207/S15326985EP3801_6) account of multimedia learning, which emphasises the benefit of combining language and visuals, supports this teaching strategy. Furthermore, the concise text and short sequences aim to help students work within the limits of processing capacity.

Classroom discussion will take place throughout the whole animation. For example, the worked animations model mathematical discovery through guided questioning. Students identify known relationships, recognise what prevents their application, and construct connections from unknown to known. As such, dynamic visualisation here represents connections, geometric reasoning and generalisation ([ten Voorde et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1093/teamat/hrae007)).

## Practice and differentiation

After each animation, students will complete procedural practice, applying the conceptual ideas. The digital resource allows the flexibility to pause and replay when students encounter difficulties. [Navarrete et al. (2025)](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40593-025-00481-x) inform the integration of animations with questions and practice. This resource similarly is not merely a tool to demonstrate knowledge, but is integrated with dynamic classroom interaction.

To satisfy different learning needs, the digital resource incorporates three difficulty levels for exercises within a shared learning sequence. The teacher suggests an initial difficulty level, and students move between levels as their understanding develops with guidance and supervision. [Suprihatiningrum and Syaripudin (2026)](https://doi.org/10.1590/1983-3652.2026.61658) identify digital platforms as tools for delivering tiered materials while emphasising the importance of the teacher’s professional judgement. Similarly, the resource provides multiple pathways while the teacher is responsible for interpreting students’ needs and adjusting the challenge and support provided.

## Digital assessment and feedback

Digital platform submission retains students’ working, explanations and subsequent revisions. The teacher’s interpretation of students’ performance, interactivity and students’ use of feedback are important considerations in mathematics e-assessment ([Iannone et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11858-026-01853-w)). As such, students are instructed to submit a solution with full working before checking the answers. The teacher then provides feedback addressing their reasoning, and students then receive the feedback and revise how to attempt the next questions. A feedback cycle is therefore formed that allows the teacher to identify common misconceptions and plan further instruction.
