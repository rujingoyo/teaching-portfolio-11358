## Part A

Hello, and welcome to my digital teaching portfolio. I will go through my Year 10 Mathematics resource, which teaches students to critically analyse an AI-generated solution. The resource is aligned with the Australian Curriculum, which emphasises factorising and simplifying algebraic expressions using mathematical laws and properties. However, I want students to both achieve the correct answers and evaluate the AI method through a discussion of correctness, purpose, explainability and efficiency.

In this question, students are asked to evaluate x+2y from a given equation. AI treated the question as a quadratic equation in x and used the discriminant to draw a connection to what is required. This solution produced the correct answer, the purpose here is not to correct AI or teach students that AI is simply unreliable. Instead, students are encouraged to retain the ability to develop their own reasoning. To achieve this, students examine the steps, verifying them and interpreting the purpose of the steps and how AI answered the question rather than trust it directly.

Students then compare the AI response with an alternative method. By suggesting factorising, students will observe the appearance of x+2y. This factorised term connects to the question directly, in contrast to the AI method that uses the discriminant unintuitively. The purpose of comparing the two methods is to recognise structure, justify equivalence and select a strategy. This is supported by research that demonstrated a complementary relationship between conceptual and procedural fluency. It is also a self-explanation activity with worked examples, which has been found effective in another study.

In addition to the mathematical learning goal, safety and human agency are also explicit. Students must follow school rules, including which AI tools or AI resources are permitted and which information should never be put into AI. Transparency is also endorsed. Students are required to record the model, prompts and the way it is used. This expectation also aligns with the Australian Framework for Generative AI in Schools. The key message in this activity is about letting students, not AI, be responsible for verification, explanation, learning and making the final decision.

This teaching resource also supports wellbeing. As students’ maths anxiety and performance are often negatively correlated, the activity aims to reduce the demand for external responses while encouraging individual thinking and preserving the challenges.

Finally, the resource addresses all three Unit Learning Outcomes, including safe, responsible and transparent AI use, a curriculum-aligned digital learning activity that develops mathematical reasoning, and structured prompts, accessible presentation and multiple ways for participation.

## Part B

Welcome to Part B of my digital teaching portfolio. This walkthrough explains my inclusive audit, the new geometry resource and how these address the unit learning outcomes. My teaching philosophy is again, conceptual understanding and procedural fluency should develop together in Mathematics.

My inclusive audit examines the Year 10 Mathematics digital citizenship activity. It identified potential barriers in explanatory prose, specialised vocabulary and unspecified response options. Universal Design for Learning guides the audit through Engagement, Representation, and Action and Expression. For Engagement, I made students’ choice of starting audit prompt explicit and provided individual thinking time before discussion. For Representation, I divided instructions into manageable sections, clarified algebraic vocabulary and placed an expandable transcript below the walkthrough video. For Action and Expression, students can now submit annotated working, a typed explanation, audio explanation or equivalent method using the same mathematical criteria. The table links to archived pages and revised sections, reveals each change.

This Year 7 geometry resource combines teacher-narrated animations, whole-class discussion, differentiated exercises and digital submission.
The curriculum links identify Year 7 learning about parallel lines and triangles. The resource prioritises the derivation of new concepts, in this case, angles, from previous learning using digital animations.

For instance, the animation first introduces supplementary angles on a straight line and derives the vertically opposite angle property. During the animation, the teacher will narrate each step and pause for predictions and explanations. For example, the worked animations model mathematical discovery through guided questioning. Students identify known relationships, recognise what prevents their application, and construct connections from unknown to known. I would pause before revealing the extra parallel line and ask what connection students need. We would then examine why that line makes known angle relationships usable. After each animation, students will complete procedural practice, applying the conceptual ideas.

To satisfy different learning needs, the digital resource incorporates three difficulty levels for exercises within a shared learning sequence. The teacher suggests an initial difficulty level, and students move between levels as their understanding develops with guidance and supervision. Similarly, the resource provides multiple pathways while the teacher is responsible for interpreting students’ needs and adjusting the challenge and support provided.

Section five establishes the Assessment in the Digital Age feedback cycle. Digital platform submission retains students’ working, explanations and subsequent revisions. The teacher then asks students to revise their solutions and explain what changed, making their developing understanding visible.
