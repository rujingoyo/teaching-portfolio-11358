Hello, and welcome to my digital teaching portfolio. I will go through my Year 10 Mathematics resource, which teaches students to critically analyse an AI-generated solution. The resource is aligned with the Australian Curriculum, which emphasises factorising and simplifying algebraic expressions using mathematical laws and properties. However, I want students to both achieve the correct answers and evaluate the AI method through a discussion of correctness, purpose, explainability and efficiency.

In this question, students are asked to evaluate x+2y from a given equation. AI treated the question as a quadratic equation in x and used the discriminant to draw a connection to what is required. This solution produced the correct answer, the purpose here is not to correct AI or teach students that AI is simply unreliable. Instead, students are encouraged to retain the ability to develop their own reasoning. To achieve this, students examine the steps, verifying them and interpreting the purpose of the steps and how AI answered the question rather than trust it directly.

Students then compare the AI response with an alternative method. By suggesting factorising, students will observe the appearance of x+2y. This factorised term connects to the question directly, in contrast to the AI method that uses the discriminant unintuitively. The purpose of comparing the two methods is to recognise structure, justify equivalence and select a strategy. This is supported by research that demonstrated a complementary relationship between conceptual and procedural fluency. It is also a self-explanation activity with worked examples, which has been found effective in another study.

In addition to the mathematical learning goal, safety and human agency are also explicit. Students must follow school rules, including which AI tools or AI resources are permitted and which information should never be put into AI. Transparency is also endorsed. Students are required to record the model, prompts and the way it is used. This expectation also aligns with the Australian Framework for Generative AI in Schools. The key message in this activity is about letting students, not AI, be responsible for verification, explanation, learning and making the final decision.

This teaching resource also supports wellbeing. As students’ maths anxiety and performance are often negatively correlated, the activity aims to reduce the demand for external responses while encouraging individual thinking and preserving the challenges.

Finally, the resource addresses all three Unit Learning Outcomes, including safe, responsible and transparent AI use, a curriculum-aligned digital learning activity that develops mathematical reasoning, and structured prompts, accessible presentation and multiple ways for participation.

## Pedagogical purpose

I chose a teaching resource that includes a correct, yet less structurally direct AI response over an incorrect hallucination. This design guides students to think in terms of clarity, efficiency and logic, rather than teaching students that the AI solution is incorrect.

[Bulut and Borromeo Ferri (2026)](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11858-026-01803-6) pointed out that students still struggle to justify or verify ChatGPT-supported mathematical modelling and exhibit uncritical acceptance. This teaching resource, therefore, sets up four dimensions: correctness, purpose, explainability and efficiency to help students analyse AI’s mathematical reasoning.

While the solution is correct, a more efficient and intuitive solution exists that uses factorising to establish a direct connection from the process (i.e. expression in terms of image) to the goal. Comparing two methods requires reasoning beyond correctness; it must also include interpretation of different conceptual understandings forming different procedural fluencies. This reflects the iterative relationship between conceptual and procedural knowledge ([Rittle-Johnson & Schneider, 2014](https://doi.org/10.1093/oxfordhb/9780199642342.013.014)). The resource promotes self-explanation, which can further improve conceptual understanding algebraically ([Booth et al., 2013](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.learninstruc.2012.11.002)). AI acts as material that encourages reasoning rather than answer authority.

## Wellbeing, inclusion and multimodal design

This activity provides psychological safety as students aim to analyse teacher-provided answers. Students are not required to share their own solutions to avoid the pressure or worry generated by uncertainty or potential mistakes, and instead, this creates a safe environment in which to participate. The four different dimensions provide various entry points. Some may commence with verifying correctness, some can interpret purposes and expressions, whereas others can initiate a discussion on efficiency. This maintains both the cognitive challenges in Year 10 and the consideration of performance and maths anxiety ([Carey et al., 2016](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2015.01987)). In addition, teacher-supplied resources also allow full participation without requiring any AI accounts.

## Safety, ethics and human agency

During the use of AI, this resource emphasises safety, transparency and responsibility. Students are required to use school- or teacher-approved AI and record information for transparency. These choices enact the Australian Framework for Generative AI in Schools principles of transparency, accountability, critical thinking, privacy and human responsibility ([Department of Education, 2023](https://www.education.gov.au/download/17416/australian-framework-generative-artificial-intelligence-ai-schools/35400/document/pdf)). Students, not AI, are responsible for the use of AI, including verification, explanation and selection.

## Unit Learning Outcomes

ULO1 is demonstrated through protecting privacy and verifying AI-generated content for responsibility, safety and ethics.

ULO2 is demonstrated through a curriculum-aligned activity addressing AC9M10A01 ([ACARA, n.d.](https://www.australiancurriculum.edu.au/f-10-curriculum/learning-areas/mathematics/year-10)), in which students use algebra to construct mathematical reasoning.

ULO3 is demonstrated through accessible multimodal design, structured engagement and a psychologically safer entry into mathematical dialogue.
