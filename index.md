## Professional statement

I am a pre-service secondary teacher specialising in Mathematics and Computing and currently completing a Master of Secondary Teaching at the University of Canberra. My practice includes curriculum-aligned planning, differentiated instruction, formative assessment and the creation of positive learning environments. I also embed ICT into the classroom to make mathematical reasoning tangible.

My teaching philosophy is that conceptual understanding and procedural fluency should develop together. Procedures provide efficiency, yet relying solely on procedures can neither connect the concept to the application nor enable students to adapt their skills in unconventional scenarios. Rittle-Johnson and Schneider (2014) found a positive relationship between conceptual understanding and procedural fluency, which complement each other. Therefore, a meaningful teaching activity invites students to observe mathematical expression structures, analyse and compare different solutions, discover the conceptual components that lie within them and therefore refine their problem-solving skills.

The Year 7 teaching resource uses animations, guided questioning and differentiated exercises to develop mathematical reasoning and procedural fluency. Students predict, explain mathematical connections and revise digitally submitted solutions after teacher feedback.

I employ the Technological Pedagogical Content Knowledge (TPACK) framework, combining subject knowledge, pedagogy and technological possibilities (Mishra & Koehler, 2006). In geometry, angle derivation and relationships in parallel lines and triangles are demonstrated using animation alone with teacher narration and guided questioning. This is designed to show how concepts emerge. Research identifies connections, reasoning and generalisation as educational roles of animated mathematics visualisations (ten Voorde et al., 2025). Students predict, justify strategies, select suitable practice, receive and act upon feedback and revise solutions. While the digital resource provides students with more opportunities for exploration, the teacher is still required to use professional judgement to determine the pace, adjust guidance, identify and correct misconceptions in a dynamic classroom. In addition, the digital platform, approved by the school, will collect students’ work to identify patterns and plan further instruction. This reflects research emphasising teacher interpretation and students’ use of feedback in mathematics e-assessment (Iannone et al., 2026). The teacher then asks students to revise their solutions and explain what changed, making their developing understanding visible.

Wellbeing in mathematical learning is highly relevant, as maths anxiety and performance are closely correlated (Carey et al., 2016). The environment I aim to create is a safe place where students can express confusion, engage in trial and error and seek help while maintaining cognitive challenges.
